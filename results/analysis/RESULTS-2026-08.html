Gradient Surgery for REPA

Projecting the conflicting component out of the alignment gradient buys two different things: a 1.47× convergence speedup on ImageNet-100, and a persistent 14% FID gain on CelebA — a dataset where alignment itself is worth nothing at all. Here is what the five runs support, what they don't, and the shortest path from here to a submittable paper.

The read

One result is strong, one is a null, and one is confounded

REPA-PCGrad is the finding. On ImageNet-100 it reaches REPA's final FID in 170k steps instead of 300k — a 1.76× step speedup, 3.33× over the plain SiT baseline — and finishes at FID 9.015 / KID 1.089×10⁻³ against REPA's 9.852 / 1.419×10⁻³. The KID gap is 23% and sits about nine combined standard errors out. This is not noise.

HASTE is a null on quality. Terminating alignment at 100k lands within noise of REPA at 300k (ΔFID −0.07, ΔKID z ≈ −0.9). Its real benefit is that the last 200k steps cost baseline compute — no encoder forward, no image read. That reframes "REPA works until it doesn't" as a cost result rather than a quality result, which is a defensible and publishable position.

CelebA is where REPA stops working — and where surgery keeps working. Baseline, REPA and HASTE plateau at KID 4.18, 4.18, 4.15: three arms agreeing to within 0.05, so alignment on its own buys nothing on a single-domain face dataset. But the preconditioned surgery arm lands at KID 3.87 and FID 6.79 — 7.3% below the baseline where REPA is 0.1% above it. Surgery converts a worthless alignment loss into a useful one. That is the most interesting single fact in the whole set.

Taken together the picture is stronger than any one run: surgery beats REPA in all eight dataset × conflict-metric × metric cells. Only which variant wins flips — Euclidean on ImageNet-100, preconditioned on CelebA — and there is a principled reason to expect exactly that.

The two results are also different kinds of result, which is easy to miss. On ImageNet-100 the advantage decays as training proceeds — a head start, worth 1.47× wall-clock. On CelebA it does not decay at all: the absolute FID gap holds at 1.14 ± 0.13 across 240k steps while the FID level itself falls 32%. That is the more valuable of the two, because it does not evaporate with more compute.

One thing stands between this and a submission: the mechanistic story — that diffusion and alignment gradients conflict at low noise — currently has zero supporting data in the repository.

Corrected for wall-clock

Surgery needs a second, partial backward pass through the alignment subgraph. The repo estimates that at ~1.2× the per-step cost of plain REPA — an estimate that is currently a comment in a README, not a measurement. Taking it at face value, the compute-matched comparison is REPA-PCGrad at 250k against REPA at 300k:

The caveat a reviewer will find first

The advantage is a head start, and it is being eaten

REPA-PCGrad KID relative to REPA negative = better · peak −56% at 75k → −23% at 300k

The FID gap tells the same story: +18.9 at 50k, +5.8 at 100k, +2.4 at 175k, +0.84 at 300k. Monotone decay after 75k, with no sign of a floor.

Any reviewer who plots the difference will see this, so plot it yourself. Framed as a quality method, the paper invites "does it still win at 1M steps?" — and on this trend the honest answer is probably not. Framed as a convergence-acceleration method, the narrowing is not a weakness at all; it is the expected signature of an optimization fix, and it is exactly what REPA itself does relative to the baseline. Own it in the abstract rather than letting it be discovered in review.

The second dataset

CelebA: alignment does nothing, surgery does

This is the result that carries the paper, and it is easy to under-read because REPA's own row is flat. The right comparison on CelebA is not surgery against REPA — it is surgery against the baseline, because REPA and the baseline are the same model here.

Precision is not the explanation

The obvious objection is the bf16/fp16 split, and CelebA answers it internally. Both surgery arms ran bf16. They differ from each other by 12.6% FID — far more than either differs from fp16 REPA on the low end. A precision effect would move them together; it doesn't. On this dataset the confound is excluded by the runs already in hand.

Why whitening should be dataset-dependent

The rank flip is a prediction, not an embarrassment, once you notice that PCGrad's Euclidean test is inherited from an SGD formulation. The update actually applied here is Adam's, P·g with P = 1/(√v̂+ε) — so "these gradients conflict" should be evaluated in the metric the optimizer moves in, which makes the whitened test the principled one and the Euclidean test the legacy one.

Whitening changes the test most where the diffusion gradient's spectrum is most anisotropic. On CelebA — one visual domain, aligned faces, 16 attribute classes — a handful of high-curvature directions dominate the Euclidean dot product, and the test goes nearly blind to the low-curvature directions where the alignment gradient lives. On ImageNet-100's 100 diverse classes the spectrum is flatter, whitening buys less, and the extra variance of estimating v̂ costs more than it gains. That predicts precisely the observed ordering, and it is directly measurable: compare the diffusion gradient's effective rank, and the agreement rate between the two conflict tests, on both datasets.

The gap does not shrink, and that is the point

Both CelebA surgery evals ran at 25k samples, the same as every other arm, so the FID numbers are directly comparable and the −13.9% stands. What is left is a genuine and rather striking property of the gap: it does not decay.

Absolute FID gap over training, indexed to its first value a quality gain shrinks as the model improves · an additive offset doesn't
CelebA — P-PCGrad vs REPA ImageNet-100 — REPA-PCGrad vs REPA

On CelebA the absolute FID gap holds at 1.14 ± 0.13 from 60k to 300k while the FID level itself falls 32% — so in relative terms it grows, 8.5% → 14%. The ImageNet gap does the opposite, decaying to 9% of its starting value. Same method family, two qualitatively different benefit profiles.

Read the two curves as two claims. ImageNet-100 is a convergence result: surgery finds the same basin sooner, and given enough steps plain REPA catches up. That is worth a speedup headline and nothing more. CelebA is a quality result: the advantage is established by 60k and then held, in absolute terms, for another 240k steps against a model that is still improving. Nothing in the trend suggests REPA closes it.

The one asymmetry worth explaining in the paper is that the CelebA gain is −13.9% in FID but −7.5% in KID, while ImageNet's is the other way round (−8.5% FID, −23.3% KID). FID is a Gaussian fit to the Inception features — first and second moments only — whereas KID's cubic kernel is sensitive to higher moments. A gain that is larger in FID than in KID is concentrated in the mean and covariance of the feature distribution: a better-centred, better-scaled match rather than a broad improvement in per-sample fidelity. That is a testable statement (decompose FID into its mean and trace terms, and inspect samples), and it is the kind of detail that makes a reviewer trust the rest of the paper.

Threats to validity

What the runs support, and what they can't

Solid

  • REPA reproduces. −32.4% FID and a 2.21× step speedup over plain SiT on ImageNet-100. The pipeline is doing what it should.
  • REPA-PCGrad beats REPA on ImageNet-100. The gap is large, opens early, holds at every one of twelve checkpoints, and survives compute matching.
  • REPA fails on CelebA. Baseline, REPA and HASTE agree to within 0.05 KID. A controlled negative result is hard to argue with.
  • Surgery works where alignment doesn't. On CelebA, P-PCGrad is 7.3% below the baseline in KID while REPA is 0.1% above it. Both surgery arms improve on REPA in all eight dataset × metric cells.
  • Precision is excluded on CelebA. Both surgery arms ran bf16 and differ from each other by 12.6% FID, so bf16 cannot be what separates P-PCGrad from the field there.
  • HASTE costs less. Post-termination steps load neither images nor encoder features. That part needs no defence.

Confounded or missing

  • Precision is uncontrolled on ImageNet-100. Both surgery arms are forced to bf16; baseline, REPA and HASTE ran fp16. At the very first checkpoint the two bf16 arms already show KID 153.2 / 154.6 against 160.9–161.2 for all three fp16 arms — a clean split along the precision line. The CelebA runs rule this out for that dataset; ImageNet-100 still needs the control run.
  • The CelebA baseline still has no FID. It was evaluated at 5k samples, so its KID is comparable (unbiased, fixed 1000-sample subsets) but no FID was written. The paper's best sentence — REPA does nothing, conflict-free REPA gives 14% — currently rests half on a row that does not exist. Eval-only fix.
  • The FID/KID asymmetry is unexplained. CelebA gains more in FID than KID; ImageNet-100 the reverse. Not a threat to either result, but a reviewer will ask, and it points at what kind of improvement each variant actually delivers.
  • Baseline eval features were never regenerated. The sweep log shows 0 of 12 checkpoints featurized for the baseline arm and 5 of 12 reused for HASTE. evaluate.py keys its cache on (step, rank) only — not on sample count, cfg, sampler steps, or --compile, which the repo's own README warns is not bitwise identical to eager.
  • n = 1 seed everywhere. The ± in every table is Inception subset noise, not run-to-run noise. Nothing here is a reason to doubt the direction of the results — every arm points the same way — but the effect sizes need seeds before they go in a table.
  • No mechanism data. The premise is that ∇ℓdiff and ∇ℓrepa conflict at low noise. No rho.csv exists anywhere in the repo. The method is currently motivated by an assertion.
  • The surgery is not isolated. When the dot product is negative the update adds a positive multiple of the EMA diffusion direction — so it is also a momentum-like term. Nothing yet separates "conflict removal" from "extra smoothed diffusion gradient".
  • Scale and protocol. ImageNet-100, SiT-B/2, FID-10K at a single cfg. None of these numbers are comparable to any published ImageNet-256 result.

Positioning

The paper this is, not the paper it looks like

"We beat REPA by 0.8 FID" is a weak paper: one dataset, one model size, one seed, a shrinking margin. The same runs support a substantially stronger claim.

Representation alignment helps a diffusion transformer only where its gradient does not fight the denoising objective. Remove the conflicting component — in the metric the optimizer actually moves in — and you recover most of the loss, including on a dataset where alignment on its own is worth exactly nothing.

Three pillars hold that up, two of which are already in hand:

The first pillar is the cheapest and the one that converts an empirical note into a mechanistic paper: training/measure_rho.py already computes ρ(t) post-hoc from existing checkpoints, no retraining. The fourth turns the variant flip from a wrinkle into a second contribution.

Next runs, in priority order

What to do this week

  1. Measure ρ(t) on the checkpoints you already have no training · hours

    Run training/measure_rho.py over the REPA and REPA-PCGrad runs, then analyze_rho.py. If cos(∇ℓdiff, ∇ℓrepa) really is positive at high noise and negative at low noise, that heatmap is the paper's Figure 1 and the whole story locks into place. If it isn't, you need a different explanation for why surgery works — better to learn that now than in review.

  2. Train REPA in bf16 1 run · 300k steps

    The single experiment most likely to be demanded and the one that most cleanly kills the biggest confound. Everything in the headline table currently compares bf16 surgery against fp16 alignment. Until this run exists, the honest claim is "REPA-PCGrad in bf16 beats REPA in fp16".

  3. Close the two eval gaps eval only · ~2 h

    Regenerate the CelebA baseline at 25k so it has an FID — the paper's best sentence ("REPA does nothing, conflict-free REPA gives 14%") is currently half-supported, with the REPA-vs-baseline half resting on KID alone. Then pass --refresh --refresh-real on the ImageNet baseline arm and HASTE's 25k–125k checkpoints, whose features came out of a cache keyed only on (step, rank) and were never regenerated under this sweep's settings.

  4. Two more seeds, on both datasets 6 runs · 150k each

    ImageNet-100 REPA and REPA-PCGrad stopped at 150k, where the FID gap is already 3.0 — you don't need 300k to establish separation. Add CelebA REPA and P-PCGrad, since that pair now carries a headline. This converts every error bar in the paper from "subset noise" to "run-to-run noise".

  5. Ablate what the surgery is actually doing 3 runs · 150k each

    Three controls that separate conflict removal from a momentum effect: (a) instantaneous ∇ℓdiff as the reference instead of its EMA; (b) always project, dropping the negative-only clamp; (c) project against a fixed random direction of matched norm — the null control. Without (c) especially, a reviewer can argue the whole effect is a smoothed gradient term wearing a PCGrad costume.

  6. Measure the gradient anisotropy that predicts the variant flip post-hoc · hours

    The hypothesis is that the whitened conflict test wins where the diffusion gradient's spectrum is concentrated and loses where it is flat. Two quantities test it directly on checkpoints you already have: the effective rank of the diffusion gradient's second moment (Adam's v̂ is sitting in the optimizer state), and the disagreement rate between the Euclidean and whitened conflict tests on the same batch. If CelebA is the anisotropic one, the flip stops being an inconvenience and becomes a rule for choosing the metric. The natural follow-up is a knob rather than a switch: interpolate the preconditioner as Pα for α ∈ {0, ½, 1} and show the optimum moving between datasets.

  7. Log how often the surgery fires cheap · add to (5)

    Record the fraction of steps with ⟨grepa, gema⟩ < 0 and the norm of the removed component, bucketed by timestep. This is a second mechanism figure for nearly no cost, and it connects directly to the ρ(t) heatmap.

  8. Fix the reporting protocol for the final table eval only

    FID-50K at the 300k checkpoint with a cfg sweep over {1.0, 1.25, 1.5, 1.75, 2.0} for every arm. FID-10K at a single cfg is fine for run-to-run curves but will not pass as a headline number, and the cfg=1.0 column is what makes your result comparable to the REPA paper's tables.

  9. Decide HASTE's role optional · 2 runs

    It is a quality null at a 100k termination step. Either sweep the termination point over {50k, 200k} to show the null is robust, or demote HASTE to a cost baseline and say plainly that its published benefit did not reproduce as a quality gain at this scale. The second option is cheaper and equally honest.

Drafting guardrails

Claims to make, claims to avoid

"Gradient surgery reaches REPA's 300k-step quality in 170k steps — a 1.47× wall-clock speedup on ImageNet-100."

Directly measurable from the curves, robust to the narrowing gap, and already compute-corrected. This is the abstract sentence.

"Gradient surgery improves FID by 8.5% over REPA."

True at 300k and shrinking monotonically since 75k. Stating it as a quality ceiling invites the one question the data cannot answer.

"Representation alignment yields no measurable benefit on CelebA: baseline, REPA and HASTE plateau within 0.05 KID×10³ of one another."

Three-arm agreement, one of the more defensible statements in the whole set. Regenerate the baseline's FID first.

"On CelebA, where alignment gives no benefit at all, conflict surgery in Adam's metric improves KID by 7.3% over the unaligned baseline and FID by 13.9% over REPA — an advantage that is established by 60k steps and does not decay."

The strongest sentence available, and the one that separates this from "REPA, tuned". Regenerate the baseline's FID so both halves rest on both metrics.

"The preconditioned variant is the better method."

It wins on CelebA and comes second on ImageNet-100. Claim the rule — the conflict test belongs in the optimizer's metric, and how much that matters tracks the gradient spectrum — and report both variants on both datasets.

"On ImageNet-100 the KID gap is roughly nine combined standard errors — but those errors are over Inception subsets, and we additionally report N seeds."

Say what the error bar measures. Reviewers notice when a suspiciously large z-score is quietly doing work it wasn't built for.

"We achieve FID 9.02 on ImageNet."

ImageNet-100, SiT-B/2, FID-10K, cfg 1.5. Every one of those qualifiers has to travel with the number, in the abstract and in the table caption.
